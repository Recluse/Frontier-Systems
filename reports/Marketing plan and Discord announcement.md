# Community Marketing and Funding Plan

This is the low-cash plan for the working-title game **Frontier Systems**. The name and feature list remain provisional until the project chooses a final title and ships a playable build. The official paid game is intended to be the primary revenue source and a visible advertisement for funding open-source projects such as DiscordPHP. Use earned attention and community participation to reach players; use donations/crowdfunding only as a secondary bridge for pre-release costs or a specific milestone.

## Ready-to-post Discord announcement

```markdown
# 🚀 Introducing Frontier Systems (working title)

We’re sharing the early direction for a cooperative engineering simulation set across the Moon, Mars, orbit, and eventually the wider Solar System.

The long-term goal is a persistent shared world where crews prospect and mine local resources, refine and combine materials, salvage machinery and wrecks, 3D-print new parts, and build those parts into habitats, machines, vehicles, ships, and orbital shipyards. Objects should have a material history: build them, operate them, repair them, take them apart, and turn their recovered materials into something new.

The game is planned for Godot, with first- and third-person play, multiplayer engineering, realistic power and life-support systems, modular construction, ship expansion, automation, and roleplay. Players could work as crew, control a Station AI or cyborg, or use optional local or remote LLM assistants. We also want an opt-in world where AI agents run the crew while people watch and advise. The simulation—not generated text—will decide what physically happens.

Our references include Take On Mars, Space Station 13, Stationeers, Space Engineers, Helion, and other engineering sandboxes. We’re building our own systems, setting, art, and fiction. The plan is to release first-party code and content under MIT and sell the official game as a way to fund work on open-source projects such as DiscordPHP. Buying the game should help support that work, and we’ll publish the revenue-allocation rule and regular reports before the paid release.

This is a long-term direction, not a list of completed features. We’re starting small: prove one habitat, one shared engineering loop, and the first mine → refine → print → recycle cycle before promising planets, fleets, or orbital industry. There is no release date yet.

The official paid release is the main funding plan, rather than recurring donation drives. We’re keeping development costs close to zero until then. For now, the most useful help is feedback and future playtesting; when the game is available, buying the official release will also support open-source work.

**What would you most want to try first?**
1. Mining, refining, and recycling materials
2. Keeping a habitat powered, pressurized, and alive
3. Designing and expanding a ship

Reply here with your choice, questions, or the engineering problem you’d like to solve in the first playable build.
```

Before posting, replace the working title if needed and add the correct project/GitHub links. Do not add a Steam link, release date, or playable-build claim until one exists.

## Positioning

**Core game promise:** *Mine it, refine it, print it, operate it, salvage it, and build something new.*

**Funding message:** *Play the simulation; help fund open-source projects like DiscordPHP.* The game should earn attention through a compelling simulation, then make the purchase-to-open-source connection easy to understand and verify.

Lead with the resource lifecycle and cooperative engineering. Shipbuilding, orbital yards, roleplay, Steam integration, Discord social features, and optional LLM agents are supporting pillars; they should not compete for equal attention in the first pitch. The story is about crews turning scarce planetary and orbital resources into a functioning industrial frontier.

The initial audiences are:

- Players of engineering, survival, space, construction, and simulation games who enjoy diagnosing interconnected systems.
- Space Station 13 and roleplay communities interested in crew stories, station roles, and controllable station AI, while respecting that the game will have original lore and content.
- Programmers, makers, Godot developers, and local-LLM enthusiasts interested in automation, open-source simulation tools, and AI-agent sandboxes.

Use real, specific examples in promotion. “A realistic engineering sandbox” is vague; “recover a damaged pump, sort its usable materials, print a replacement seal, and restore the habitat loop” is demonstrable.

## Low-cash marketing and flagship funding model

### Channels

| Channel | Purpose | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Discord | Home for announcements, questions, playtests, and crew feedback | One substantial update every two weeks; use smaller posts when there is something concrete |
| GitHub | Source, issues, roadmap, changelog, licensing, and technical notes | Update with every meaningful code milestone; keep public issues scoped and current |
| YouTube | Searchable gameplay/devlog archive and longer demonstrations | One short, edited milestone video when there is a playable result |
| One short-form/social channel | Reach people who do not already follow the project | Reuse a short clip and a clear caption from each milestone; do not attempt to maintain every platform |
| Relevant game/dev communities | Find testers and collaborators already interested in the mechanics | Participate helpfully; post only where community rules allow promotion |
| Steam | Official commercial page, demo, launch, and event participation | Open the page when the hook and visual proof are credible; publish a demo when it is stable |

Prefer a project-owned README/landing page and Discord invite as the early call to action. Do not chase social-platform trends or buy ads while the game still lacks a vertical slice.

### Marketing from work already done

Each finished milestone should yield a small reusable content kit:

1. One 15–45 second in-engine clip showing a complete action and its consequence.
2. Two screenshots with concise annotations or labels.
3. A short devlog explaining the problem, the implementation choice, and what players can now do.
4. One question for the community and one link to the relevant issue, playtest, or project page.

This keeps marketing from becoming a separate art production project. Use footage from real builds, authored diagrams, project-owned SVG/typography, and geometry already made for the game. Never imply a mock-up is gameplay. Record AI-assisted asset provenance and make sure the project can distribute accepted first-party material under MIT; do not use game screenshots, logos, music, or art from reference titles as promotional assets.

### Milestone campaign

**1. Project discovery:** post the announcement above; collect responses about the first engineering loop, preferred roles, and must-have accessibility options. Add a public roadmap with `planned`, `in progress`, and `playable` states. Introduce the paid game as the intended way to support open-source work, without asking people to pay before there is a product to buy.

**2. Vertical-slice proof:** publish a monthly or fortnightly clip of the same loop improving over time: resource prospecting, extraction, material processing, 3D printing, repair, recycling, and multiplayer state agreement. Share failures and changed decisions; trust is more valuable than pretending every prototype is polished.

**3. Small playtests:** invite interested community members in manageable cohorts. Give each test a specific question, a short build, and an exit survey. Publish what changed after the test. Do not promise public persistence, server uptime, or a broad content schedule until the service can sustain them.

**4. Steam presence:** publish the base-game page once the title, capsule art, tags, feature scope, and genuine gameplay video are coherent. Wishlists can notify interested players at launch, Early Access exit, and qualifying discounts, but Steam says wishlists generally do not affect algorithmic visibility. Optimize the demo and player experience first; use Steam's traffic reporting to learn where visits come from. ([Steamworks: Visibility on Steam](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/visibility?l=english))

**5. Steam Next Fest:** treat it as a one-shot campaign, not an automatic calendar event. Steam's current rules require an upcoming, unreleased title with a public base-game page and a publicly playable demo, and permit a game to participate once. Choose it only when the demo is stable and the team can handle feedback and support; recheck the event-specific dates and build-review deadlines. ([Steamworks: Steam Next Fest](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest?l=english))

**6. Release:** build a simple press kit from the final name, description, screenshots, trailer, logo, developer contact, license/source links, a one-sentence open-source funding message, and a clear account of what works in the current release. Put the purchase-to-funding explanation on the Steam page, trailer end card, website, credits/about screen, and release posts. Contact a small, relevant list of creators and outlets with personal notes and a playable build. Never buy a large generic contact list or send mass unsolicited messages.

## Fundraising with a skeleton budget

### Game sales fund the open-source ecosystem

Make official game sales the primary open-source funding engine. Maintain a distinct open-source budget line and publish a simple rule for how net receipts support DiscordPHP and other named projects. Before the first paid release, decide whether the contribution is a percentage of net receipts, a fixed quarterly amount, or a share of surplus after a capped game-maintenance reserve. Define net receipts after refunds, taxes, platform/payment fees, and other deductions; describe how funds are allocated among projects; and publish an aggregated report on a regular schedule. Do not claim that “all proceeds” or a fixed percentage goes to open source until the policy is chosen and can be followed.

Make the funding relationship visible along the customer journey: storefront description and trailer end card, project landing page, credits/about screen, major release announcements, and quarterly funding reports. Explain that the money supports maintainer time, compatibility work, issue response, security fixes, and infrastructure. The game's DiscordPHP integration showcases the software, but the game itself must lead as a worthwhile product. Do not promise exclusive code/assets, gameplay advantages, or charity/tax treatment.

### Pre-release funding asks

Use sponsorship, donations, or crowdfunding as a secondary bridge for pre-release costs or a specific milestone—not as the long-term funding model. Keep a public “what funding unlocks” page with four fields: **blocker, amount, quote/source, and deliverable**. Example: “We need `$X` for one month of test-server capacity so 20 invited testers can run the persistent-world stress test; if we do not raise it, the test moves to a local volunteer-hosted session.” Use actual quotes and costs when known; do not invent targets in advance.

Recruit in-kind help when it can genuinely reduce pre-release costs: QA, translation, accessibility review, documentation, community moderation, hardware loans, or hosting credits. Define the task, expected hours, attribution, and license terms clearly. Do not assume volunteer labor will materialize, and do not make free contributors responsible for production-critical operations without a backup.

For money, start with one contribution route rather than several. GitHub Sponsors is suited to supporting an open-source contributor or project and can link from the repository; eligibility depends on supported locations and platform terms. Open Collective is worth considering if the project wants shared, public income/expense reporting, but review the chosen fiscal host, platform contribution flow, fees, legal/tax setup, and payout terms first. ([GitHub Sponsors documentation](https://docs.github.com/en/sponsors/receiving-sponsorships-through-github-sponsors/about-github-sponsors-for-open-source-contributors), [Open Collective pricing](https://opencollective.com/organizations/pricing))

Keep donations separate from access to MIT-licensed code and content. Offer modest acknowledgements, progress reports, or optional test participation only when the team can deliver them; do not promise exclusive rights to code/assets, gameplay advantages, or a release date. Consider crowdfunding only after a playable prototype, a costed milestone, rights review, and a realistic plan for fulfillment—the campaign itself takes time and creates obligations.

## Metrics and monthly review

Track a small set of useful measures:

- Number of people who give actionable feedback and return for another playtest.
- Demo start/completion and return-session rates, once a demo exists.
- Discord joins by source and the share of new members who engage with a playtest or feedback prompt.
- Relevant creator coverage and resulting qualified visits/testers.
- Steam page traffic, wishlists, demo traffic, and their source, once the page is public.
- Donations received, fees, expenses, and which stated milestone they funded.
- Official-game net receipts, open-source allocations by project, and the date/period covered by each public report.

Review once a month or after each major milestone. If a channel produces views but no discussion, playtests, or store interest, reduce effort there. Keep the public update cadence sustainable; do not create a marketing promise that competes with building the game.
