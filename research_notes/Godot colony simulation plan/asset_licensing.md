# Open/free asset policy for Frontier Systems

Research date: 2026-10-09. This is a project policy recommendation, not legal advice.

## 1. Which licenses should the project accept, and how should it handle compatibility?

### Takeaway

Use project-created art, models, textures, audio, code, and plugins only when they can be distributed under the project's MIT license. For third-party creative assets, use CC0 as the default. This repository's commercial and MIT-reuse goals are best served by excluding CC BY, CC BY-SA, and other differently licensed creative assets from shipped builds unless the project owner changes this policy explicitly. Use individually approved permissive software licenses for external code and plugins, and preserve their license notices.

### Cited Findings

- CC0 is a public-domain dedication (with a fallback license in jurisdictions where waiver is not effective), while CC BY allows commercial reuse and adaptations subject to attribution; CC BY-SA adds a requirement to license shared adaptations on the same terms. Creative Commons recommends its 4.0 suite and does not recommend CC licenses for software. [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [CC license descriptions](https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/cclicenses/), [CC0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/legalcode), [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en), [CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en)
- CC BY 4.0 requires retaining supplied creator/copyright/license/disclaimer/source information, marking changes, and linking to the license; its legal code allows attribution to be supplied in a reasonable way for the medium and context. [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)
- CC BY-SA 4.0 requires that a license applied to an adaptation not prevent recipients from complying with CC BY-SA. Creative Commons describes the share-alike obligation as applying to adaptations, not as a blanket relicensing rule for every work in a larger collection. The classification of a particular game asset as an adaptation or a separate/combined work is a context-specific legal question; do not assume that merely putting an asset in a game automatically resolves it either way. [CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en), [CC license descriptions](https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/cclicenses/)
- Creative Commons licenses do not grant trademark, patent, privacy, or publicity rights; a license can therefore be valid for the copyright in an asset while other rights still matter. [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)
- MIT permits use, modification, distribution, sublicensing, and sale, provided the copyright and permission notice are included in copies or substantial portions. Apache-2.0 likewise allows redistribution and derivatives, and additionally requires the license, notices for changed files, preservation of relevant notices, and propagation of any applicable NOTICE text. MIT, BSD-2-Clause, BSD-3-Clause, and Apache-2.0 are OSI-approved licenses. [OSI MIT text](https://opensource.org/license/mit), [Apache-2.0 text](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0), [OSI approved license list](https://opensource.org/licenses)
- Godot Engine is MIT-licensed; its project says the game content created with Godot is separately owned/licensed, but a distributed Godot binary requires its copyright/license notice in documentation or credits. [Godot license](https://godotengine.org/license/)
- The FSF explains that GPL-covered software cannot be incorporated into a proprietary system under the ordinary GPL grant, while genuinely separate programs may be distributed side-by-side when they remain separate in substance. Whether a particular combination is one covered work is not decided just by how a repository is organized. [GNU GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.en.html)
- SIL OFL 1.1 is specifically designed for font software. The license permits bundling fonts in apps; if a font is modified and distributed, the OFL conditions apply, including any Reserved Font Name requirements. [Official OFL text](https://openfontlicense.org/open-font-license-official-text/), [OFL FAQ](https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/), [Google Fonts developer page](https://developers.google.com/fonts)

### Inferences

- **Project policy for shipped creative assets:** MIT for project-created material and CC0 for third-party art, models, textures, and audio. Record provenance even for CC0 files. CC BY 4.0 permits commercial reuse with attribution, but it is not the selected default because its license differs from the project's MIT-first content policy; do not ship it without an explicit policy change.
- **Assets to reject by default:** CC BY-NC, CC BY-ND, CC BY-NC-SA, CC BY-NC-ND, “free for personal use,” “editorial use only,” marketplace-only licenses, terms that prohibit redistribution in game packages, and assets with no verifiable license. NC is incompatible with a possible commercial release; ND is a poor fit for the normal import, conversion, recoloring, cropping, remix, and packaging work of a game asset pipeline. Obtain a separate written permission if there is a compelling exception.
- **Other creative licenses:** treat CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC BY-ND, and other non-CC0 licenses as excluded from shipped creative content under the current policy. A license review can explain rights and obligations, but adding such content still requires the project owner to explicitly change the policy.
- **Code and plugins:** project-owned code should use one stated OSI-approved license (MIT or Apache-2.0 are straightforward candidates). New dependencies should preferably be MIT, BSD-2-Clause, BSD-3-Clause, or Apache-2.0, with license texts/notices retained. Put GPL/AGPL dependencies, code copied from copyleft projects, and code with “source available”/custom licenses on an exception queue; do not let AI-generated code merge until its origin and license are understood. LGPL/MPL dependencies need a file/combined-work review rather than being silently treated as permissive.
- **Fonts:** accept only first-party MIT or third-party CC0 fonts under the current policy. Do not add SIL OFL or another differently licensed font unless the project owner explicitly changes the policy; a license review should still check any font-specific redistribution and name restrictions.
- Keep art licenses separate from code licenses: use MIT for first-party creative assets, CC0 for third-party creative assets, and an approved software license for external scripts, shaders, plugins, and tools. This makes each reused file's rights clear and avoids using Creative Commons licenses as a substitute for a software license.

### Gaps

- There is no general bright-line answer in these sources for whether any particular embedded/repacked game asset creates a share-alike adaptation obligation; have counsel assess any high-value CC BY-SA asset or use a simpler-license substitute. [CC BY-SA 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/legalcode.en)
- These recommendations do not determine rights under every country’s copyright, moral-rights, trademark, privacy, publicity, or consumer-protection law. Creative Commons itself says its FAQ is informational and not legal advice. [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)

## 2. Which asset sources and creation methods fit the project?

### Takeaway

Prioritize procedural and self-authored low-complexity art, with curated CC0 packs for starter geometry/materials and CC0 audio. Treat source websites as discovery routes only: approve a specific downloaded file after checking the exact item’s license, attribution, and embedded dependencies.

### Cited Findings

- Kenney states that its game assets on asset pages are CC0, usable commercially, and do not require attribution; it asks users not to use its logo except for official projects. [Kenney support/licensing FAQ](https://kenney.nl/support)
- Poly Haven states that its HDRIs, textures, and 3D models are CC0 and usable commercially without required attribution. Its site terms distinguish CC0 asset files from protected site material such as logos, text, and example renders. [Poly Haven license](https://polyhaven.com/license), [Poly Haven logo guidelines](https://polyhaven.com/logo)
- Freesound hosts individual user-uploaded sounds under different licenses; its FAQ identifies CC0, CC BY, and CC BY-NC, notes that users upload the files, and provides a filter for CC0/CC BY “Free Cultural Works” sounds. It warns that an uploader may have uploaded material without rights. [Freesound FAQ](https://freesound.org/help/faq/)
- NASA says its content, including media used in 3D models such as texture maps and polygon data, is generally not subject to U.S. copyright, but some NASA-site imagery is third-party copyrighted; NASA insignia/logotypes/identifiers are protected; identifiable people and implied endorsement create separate constraints. [NASA Images and Media Usage Guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)
- U.S. Copyright Office guidance says AI-output copyrightability is a fact-specific inquiry; in the U.S., prompts alone under currently generally available systems do not provide sufficient human control for authorship, while meaningful human contributions may protect the human-authored contribution or work as a whole. [Copyright Office, Part 2: Copyrightability](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- The Copyright Office’s May 2025 pre-publication Part 3 report describes AI training copyright as an active debate with pending lawsuits, and says its analysis is limited to then-current circumstances and public information. [Copyright Office, Part 3: Generative AI Training](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf), [Copyright Office AI report index](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/)
- OSI’s Open Source AI Definition 1.0 calls for data information that describes training-data provenance, scope, collection/selection, labeling, processing/filtering, and where public and third-party data can be obtained; it also calls for training/run code and model parameters under qualifying terms. [OSI Open Source AI Definition 1.0](https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-definition)

### Inferences

- **Approved-source shortlist:**
  - **Kenney** for blockout models, simple props, UI primitives, icons, and prototype kits. Use each asset-page pack’s included license file; do not copy the site logo or promotional images into the game.
  - **Poly Haven** for realistic surface materials, HDRIs, and selected 3D props where the source asset fits the game’s style and scale. Download the asset itself, not its marketing render or site branding.
  - **Freesound** only with an item-level CC0 license check under the current project policy. Exclude CC BY, CC BY-NC, and older/unclear terms; save the sound’s creator, item URL, exact license/version, and the item-page evidence. Don't treat the platform-wide license as the sound license.
  - **NASA** as a supplemental source for factual planetary reference/media only after checking the specific caption and rights markings. Avoid NASA marks and identifiable-person imagery in product art and marketing. “NASA-hosted” alone is not an asset license.
  - **Google Fonts** may be used as a font discovery source, but confirm and record the exact family’s license file; prefer OFL. Google states fonts listed through its service are under open-source licenses, but that umbrella statement does not replace the per-font license record. [Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts), [OFL official text](https://openfontlicense.org/open-font-license-official-text/)
- **Procedural/self-authored art plan:** produce a coherent visual identity from code-authored geometry and material rules: modular habitat panels, airlocks, trusses, handrails, pipes, cables, warning markings, industrial decals, control-panel layouts, and terrain tiling. Store mesh/material generators and parameters as code; version the generated source (or generator plus seed) alongside the exported Godot asset. Use a small palette, consistent silhouette language, and configurable wear/dust states so the result looks designed without requiring bespoke hand-painted art. This is a production recommendation, not an assertion that every procedural output is automatically free of third-party rights.
- **AI-generated visual/audio assets:** use generation first for internal reference boards, layout ideas, or temporary placeholders. A final shipped AI-assisted asset needs the same rights review as any other asset: tool terms must allow commercial use, redistribution, and the project’s chosen open license; the inputs must be assets the team may submit; no user-uploaded reference or style imitation of a living artist/known franchise without clearance; record model/provider, model/version or checkpoint, prompt/settings/seed, dates, input asset IDs, edits, and generated output hash. Do not claim that a prompt alone makes an output project-owned or copyrightable. Prefer substantial human-authored edits or procedural reconstruction for final assets, and only publish an open license over rights the project actually holds.
- **AI model/training risk:** “open weights,” a permissive inference license, or a model provider’s output-use promise does not establish that training data has complete provenance or that every generated output is unique/clear. When a model vendor does not supply useful training-data information, treat provenance as unknown and restrict that model to internal ideation or reject it for production asset generation.
- **Avoid dependence on difficult visual asset production:** keep simulation content legible through functional interfaces, labels, diagrams, color-coded systems, emissive states, and modular industrial forms; reserve advanced character animation and unique creature/vehicle art for later milestones after the core sim proves out.

### Gaps

- The source list is not an asset catalog. Each individual pack/model/texture/sound/font must be checked for its current item-specific license and any bundled third-party components before use.
- NASA’s guidance is U.S.-copyright-oriented, and the page notes separate restrictions on trademarks, publicity/privacy and endorsement; it is not a universal clearance for every image hosted on NASA websites. [NASA Images and Media Usage Guidelines](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)
- The Copyright Office reports are U.S. analysis and the training report cited here is marked pre-publication. They do not decide the rights for a specific provider, dataset, model, or output. [Part 2](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf), [Part 3 pre-publication report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)

## 3. How should provenance, attribution, and review work in the AI-first pipeline?

### Takeaway

Make asset approval a small, auditable intake step that AI coding agents cannot bypass. Every imported file gets an immutable provenance record and human review before it enters the production asset directory; automated checks can catch missing records/notices, but cannot decide whether a license grant is valid or third-party rights are clear.

### Cited Findings

- CC recommends clearly marking what material is covered, including a link to the license deed, and using an appropriate attribution; CC BY 4.0 specifies that creators can request source links, change indications, and license notices. [CC marking guidance and FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/), [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)
- SPDX provides stable license identifiers and expressions to label file-level licensing and reduce ambiguous descriptions; it says a top-level license alone can be hard for downstream users to interpret when a file is separated from the repository. [SPDX handling license information](https://spdx.dev/learn/handling-license-info/)
- REUSE’s specification aims for comprehensive, unambiguous, human- and machine-readable copyright/licensing information for each file. [REUSE Specification](https://reuse.software/spec/)
- CC licenses are irrevocable once granted, but CC notes the licensor must own/control rights and the licenses only grant rights the licensor has authority to grant. [CC licensing guidance](https://creativecommons.org/cc-license-your-work/), [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/)
- MIT requires notices to accompany redistributed copies/substantial portions; Apache-2.0 requires the license and preservation of relevant notices and any included NOTICE text. [MIT](https://opensource.org/license/mit), [Apache-2.0](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0)

### Inferences

- Add `THIRD_PARTY_ASSETS.csv` or an equivalent checked-in manifest with one row per distinct file (not just per pack):
  `asset_id`, `project_path`, `title`, `creator`, `source_page_url`, `direct_download_url`, `download_date`, `source_version`, `license_spdx_or_cc_id`, `license_version`, `license_url`, `required_credit`, `modification_summary`, `source_sha256`, `shipped_sha256`, `tool/model/prompt` when relevant, `reviewer`, `approval_date`, and any restrictions/notes. Keep the exact license text or packaged license file where redistribution terms permit, and retain a local evidence snapshot/hash for the item page because discovery pages can change.
- Keep the manifest paired with `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md`, a `licenses/` directory, and an in-game or packaged credits/attributions view. Generate the credits view from the manifest where practical; have the release gate fail for a missing entry, missing license text, unknown license ID, or unmet attribution field. Include Godot’s MIT notice in distributed documentation/credits. [Godot license](https://godotengine.org/license/)
- For code, use `SPDX-License-Identifier` and copyright headers on new files where appropriate, plus dependency/license scanning on each release candidate. For art and audio, use a manifest entry per file and an attribution manifest export; automated scanning will not reliably recognize all imported binary assets.
- **Human intake checklist:** (1) identify exact file and creator; (2) verify the asset’s own license, not only the collection/site; (3) confirm commercial use, modification, redistribution, attribution, and DRM/technical restrictions; (4) inspect included sub-assets, logos, people, brands, or sample content; (5) record source/license evidence and modifications; (6) reject creative assets whose license is not MIT for first-party material or CC0 for third-party material unless the project owner explicitly changes policy; (7) approve or reject; (8) run packaged build/credits checks before release.
- **AI agent workflow:** agents can suggest candidates, create manifest drafts, run SPDX/REUSE checks, and prepare attribution text. They must not download-and-commit an unreviewed asset, infer “free” from search snippets, mint a license label for an AI output, or overwrite an asset’s source/license record. Require code review of the manifest diff along with the binary diff.
- Ask contributors and AI-assisted contributors to submit original work or assets whose rights they can document. Record that the contributor has authority to offer the asset under the proposed project license; if they cannot make that representation, do not accept the asset. This follows CC’s own warning that only a copyright holder or authorized person should apply a license. [CC license your work guidance](https://creativecommons.org/cc-license-your-work/)

### Gaps

- A manifest, hash, or stored license copy documents the team’s review; none of these independently proves the uploader owned all rights or resolves a later dispute. CC licenses include no general warranty of title/non-infringement. [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)
- License scanners do not determine whether a creative work is an adaptation, whether a contributor has authority to license it, or whether trademark/publicity/privacy rights are cleared. Reserve human review (and legal counsel for material exceptions) for those questions.
